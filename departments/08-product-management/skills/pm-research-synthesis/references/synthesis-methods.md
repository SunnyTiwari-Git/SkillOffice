# Research synthesis methods

## Thematic analysis

1. Familiarize: read everything before coding.
2. Code: tag each observation or quote with descriptive codes. Generous codes are easier to merge than split.
3. Develop themes: group related codes.
4. Review: does each theme have enough evidence, and are themes distinct?
5. Refine: name each theme and describe it in 1 or 2 sentences.
6. Report themes as findings with evidence.

## Affinity grouping

- One observation per note; let clusters emerge rather than pre-defining categories.
- Split clusters that grow too large; outliers are interesting, do not force them in.

## Triangulation

- Methods: the same question answered by interviews, surveys and analytics.
- Sources: the same method across different segments.
- Time: the same observation at different points in time.

## Interview notes

- Separate behaviors from attitudes; note context (when, where, how often).
- Workarounds are unmet needs in disguise.
- A quote is evidence, not a finding. The finding is the interpretation.
- Signals of intensity: emotional language, frequency, effort spent on workarounds, consequences.

## Survey data

- Check response rate and possible non-response bias.
- Look at distributions, not only averages (a 3.5 average can hide a split of 1s and 5s).
- Segment results; aggregates hide differences.
- Small samples and small differences are noise; do not over-interpret.
- Likert scales are ordinal, not interval data.
- Correlation in cross-tabs is not causation.
- Code open-ended answers like mini interviews.

## Qualitative and quantitative together

- Qualitative explains what and why; quantitative shows how much and how many.
- Use quantitative data to prioritize qualitative themes, and qualitative data to explain quantitative surprises.
- When they disagree, check populations, stated versus actual behavior, and question wording. Report the disagreement.

## Evidence-based persona template

Build personas only from behavioral clusters in the data; 3 to 5 at most.

```
[Persona name]: [one-line description]
Who they are: role, organization type and size, experience
Goals and jobs to be done:
How they use the product: frequency, key workflows, tools used alongside
Top pain points and workarounds:
What they value; what would make them switch:
Representative quotes (attributed to participant type):
Segment size (only if data supports it):
```

Common mistakes: demographic instead of behavioral personas, too many personas, fictional personas, personas that change no decision.

## Opportunity sizing

- Estimate affected users, frequency, severity and business effect (retention, upgrades, acquisition).
- Impact is roughly users affected x frequency x severity; also weigh evidence strength, strategic fit and feasibility.
- Show the math and assumptions, use ranges instead of false precision, and rank opportunities against each other.
